Welcome to Plants!

Plants grow, compete for room, evolve, and die in a flat arena. There is no landscape here and no weather — the ground is the same everywhere. The only thing that makes one spot different from another is what is already growing in it.

That turns out to be enough, and it is the whole idea. A plant is not adapted to a climate; it is adapted to a situation. Five of them, and each one is a way to make a living:

  • Open ground. Pioneers race into gaps and suffocate when the gap closes over them.
  • Its own thicket. Some plants do best pressed against themselves, building a dense mat — but a new one standing alone has almost none of that around it and has to survive on very little until its second node arrives.
  • A stand of its own kind. Others grow best among plants pursuing the same strategy, in company rather than alone.
  • A contested border. Some make their living precisely where they are pressed against strangers.
  • Standing dead wood. And some live on what the others leave behind.

Those five make each other, in a cycle nobody wrote down. Pioneers fill the open ground, which makes crowding, which is what a thicket-builder wants; a thicket ages into dead wood, which is what the wood-dwellers want; the wood rots through and the gap is back. Watch one patch for a while and you can see it go round.

Colour is information, not decoration. A plant's hue says which of the five it is built for — yellow-green for open ground, dark green for its own thicket, teal for a stand of its kind, rust for a border, bone for dead wood. How vivid it is says how committed it is: generalists wash out toward grey. So the picture is a map of what every patch is doing, and when the same colour appears twice in similar places, that is convergent evolution and not a coincidence.

What a plant inherits is a short program, not a set of numbers. Every node runs it to decide how many children it may have, how far away to put them, which way to turn, how fast to grow up, and how much it wants to grow right now. Because the program can read where the node finds itself, one plant can carry both kinds of node at once — nurseries that sit and reproduce, scouts that run. If you cannot see both inside a single plant, the system is not working, whatever the diversity numbers say.

Plants come apart. A node that dies while still holding children does not vanish: it stops growing and stays as structure, which is what heartwood is in a real trunk too. Eventually it rots through, and whatever was hanging off it becomes a plant in its own right. That is how new individuals mostly arrive here — not from seed, but by bodies breaking apart where they already stand.

The arena is 16 by 9 and it has edges. Whatever shape your window is, the world is the same world; the black bars are outside it. Growth is simply refused at the frame, so a plant pressed against the edge loses the turns it spends pushing. Nothing wraps around to the other side.

The graphs tell you whether it is still working. Two of them carry a dashed line, and those two are the whole test: if life stops being spread across the five niches, or if one strategy takes more than half the world, the arena has gone to monoculture and the piece has failed. Everything else on that page is description.

Watching

  • Press a plant to highlight the whole of it and count its nodes. Drag across the arena to sweep from one to the next.
  • A plant is often larger than it looks: two or three patches of living growth strung together on a thread of dead wood are one organism, and the highlight takes all of it.
  • The magenta rings mark the biggest plants in the world.

Sharing

The settings page has a link you can copy to share your world and its settings. A link always starts its world from the beginning, so leave it running to reach what you were looking at. And if you changed settings part way through, it will not even do that — what you were watching took a path that a list of settings cannot describe. The panel tells you which of the two you have, underneath the link.
